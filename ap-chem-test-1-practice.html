AP Chemistry Test 1 — Practice Set & Reference

Significant figures · Percent composition · Mole calculations · Empirical formulas · Net ionic equations · Alloys · Mass spectrometry · Labs: unknown chloride, hydrate

The five mistakes that cost the most points

  1. Rounding mid-problem. Carry extra digits, round once at the very end.
  2. Dividing by the wrong smallest number in empirical formulas — it must be the smallest mole value.
  3. Leaving out states (s, l, g, aq) in net ionic equations. Usually a required point.
  4. Forgetting subscripts when counting atoms: one mole of \(\ce{Al2(SO4)3}\) has 12 mol O, not 4.
  5. Using mass fractions as if they were mole fractions in mass spec. Weight by abundance, not by mass.

1. Significant figures

Counting them

  • All nonzero digits count. \(4521\) → 4 sig figs.
  • Zeros between nonzeros count. \(4.005\) → 4.
  • Leading zeros never count. \(0.00420\) → 3 (the 4, the 2, and the trailing 0).
  • Trailing zeros count only if there's a decimal point. \(50.0\) → 3; \(1200\) → 2 (ambiguous); \(1200.\) → 4.
  • Scientific notation removes all ambiguity: \(1.200\times10^{3}\) → 4.
  • Exact numbers (counted objects, defined conversions like \(1\ \mathrm{m}=100\ \mathrm{cm}\)) have infinite sig figs and never limit your answer.

Carrying them through math

\[\times \ \text{and}\ \div \ \longrightarrow\ \text{answer takes the \textbf{fewest significant figures}}\] \[+ \ \text{and}\ - \ \longrightarrow\ \text{answer takes the \textbf{fewest decimal places}}\]

Mixed operations: apply the rule at each step's level, but only actually round at the end.

Watch for: molar masses. Use at least as many sig figs as your data has, or you manufacture rounding error. Use \(18.02\ \mathrm{g/mol}\) for water, not \(18\).

2. Mole calculations

\[n=\frac{m}{M}\qquad N=n\,N_{A}\qquad M=\frac{m}{n}\] moles = grams ÷ molar mass  ·  particles = moles × \(6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}\)  ·  molar mass = grams ÷ moles

Everything is one road map. To get from any quantity to any other, walk it:

\[\text{grams}\ \underset{\times M}{\overset{\div M}{\rightleftharpoons}}\ \boxed{\text{MOLES}}\ \underset{\div N_A}{\overset{\times N_A}{\rightleftharpoons}}\ \text{particles}\] \[\boxed{\text{MOLES}}\ \xrightarrow{\ \times\ \text{subscript}\ }\ \text{moles of one element}\]

Moles is always the hub. You can never go grams → particles directly.

Dimensional analysis, done right

Write every conversion as a fraction with units, and cancel. If the units don't cancel to what you want, the setup is wrong — check that before you press a single calculator key.

\[2.50\ \mathrm{g}\ \ce{H2O}\times\frac{1\ \mathrm{mol}}{18.02\ \mathrm{g}}\times\frac{6.022\times10^{23}\ \text{molecules}}{1\ \mathrm{mol}}=8.35\times10^{22}\ \text{molecules}\]
Sanity check every answer. Fewer than about \(100\ \mathrm{g}\) of anything is usually a few moles at most. If you get \(10^{5}\) moles from 12 grams, you divided the wrong way.

3. Percent composition

\[\%\,\text{element}=\frac{\text{mass of that element in 1 mol}}{\text{molar mass of compound}}\times100\]

Use the total mass of the element, so multiply by the subscript. Percentages must sum to 100% (± rounding) — that's your free check.

Worked shape

%N in \(\ce{NH4NO3}\): two N per formula unit, so \(2(14.01)=28.02\ \mathrm{g}\), and the molar mass is \(80.05\ \mathrm{g/mol}\).

\[\%\,\ce{N}=\frac{28.02}{80.05}\times100=35.00\%\]

The reverse direction

Convert the percent to a decimal first (35.00% → 0.3500), then:

\[\text{element mass}=(\text{decimal})\times(\text{sample mass})\qquad \text{sample needed}=\frac{\text{element mass}}{\text{decimal}}\]

Fertilizer and ore problems always run one of these two ways.

4. Empirical & molecular formulas

Empirical formula = simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. Molecular formula = the actual count, always a whole-number multiple of the empirical.

The five-step algorithm (memorize this)

  1. Percent to mass. Assume exactly \(100\ \mathrm{g}\), so 40.0% C becomes \(40.0\ \mathrm{g}\) C. (If you're given masses already, skip this.)
  2. Mass to moles. Divide each by its atomic mass.
  3. Divide by the smallest mole value.
  4. Multiply to whole numbers if needed. Only round when you're within about \(0.1\) of an integer.
  5. Write the formula using those numbers as subscripts.

Decimals you must not round away

You getMultiply everything byExample
\(.50\)2\(1:1.50\ \rightarrow\ 2:3\)
\(.33\) or \(.67\)3\(1:1.33\ \rightarrow\ 3:4\)
\(.25\) or \(.75\)4\(1:2.25\ \rightarrow\ 4:9\)
\(.20,.40,.60,.80\)5\(1:1.40\ \rightarrow\ 5:7\)

Getting the molecular formula

\[n=\frac{\text{molar mass of compound}}{\text{empirical formula mass}}\qquad \text{molecular formula}=(\text{empirical formula})\times n\]

\(\ce{CH2O}\) has an empirical mass of \(30.03\). If the molar mass is \(180.\ \mathrm{g/mol}\), then \(n=180./30.03\approx6\), giving \(\ce{C6H12O6}\). Note that \(n\) rounds to a clean integer; if yours doesn't, recheck the empirical formula.

Combustion variant: if you're given the masses of \(\ce{CO2}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\) produced, then \(n_{\ce{C}}=n_{\ce{CO2}}\) and \(n_{\ce{H}}=2\,n_{\ce{H2O}}\). Find the mass of O by subtracting the C and H masses from the original sample mass — never by assuming.

5. Net ionic equations

The procedure

  1. Write the balanced molecular equation with correct formulas and states.
  2. Write the complete ionic equation: split every strong electrolyte that is \((aq)\) into its ions.
  3. Cancel the spectator ions (identical on both sides, same state).
  4. What's left is the net ionic equation. Check that both mass and charge balance.

What splits and what doesn't

Strong bases: group 1 hydroxides, plus \(\ce{Ca(OH)2}\), \(\ce{Sr(OH)2}\), \(\ce{Ba(OH)2}\) Weak bases: \(\ce{NH3}\), amines

Solubility rules

Always solubleGroup 1 (\(\ce{Li+}\), \(\ce{Na+}\), \(\ce{K+}\)…), \(\ce{NH4+}\), \(\ce{NO3-}\), \(\ce{CH3COO-}\), \(\ce{ClO4-}\) — no exceptions worth memorizing
Soluble, with exceptions \(\ce{Cl-}\), \(\ce{Br-}\), \(\ce{I-}\) — except \(\ce{Ag+}\), \(\ce{Pb^2+}\), \(\ce{Hg2^2+}\)
\(\ce{SO4^2-}\) — except \(\ce{Ba^2+}\), \(\ce{Pb^2+}\), \(\ce{Ca^2+}\), \(\ce{Sr^2+}\), \(\ce{Ag+}\)
Insoluble, with exceptions \(\ce{CO3^2-}\), \(\ce{PO4^3-}\), \(\ce{S^2-}\), \(\ce{OH-}\) — except with group 1 or \(\ce{NH4+}\)
\(\ce{S^2-}\) is also soluble with group 2 (\(\ce{CaS}\), \(\ce{SrS}\), \(\ce{BaS}\))
\(\ce{OH-}\): \(\ce{Ba(OH)2}\) and \(\ce{Sr(OH)2}\) are soluble enough to treat as strong bases; \(\ce{Ca(OH)2}\) is only slightly soluble

Three reaction types that drive a reaction

  • Precipitation — an insoluble solid forms: \(\ce{Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq) -> AgCl(s)}\)
  • Neutralization — water forms. Strong + strong: \(\ce{H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)}\)
  • Gas-forming — carbonates with acid give \(\ce{CO2}\); sulfides give \(\ce{H2S}\):
    \(\ce{CO3^2-(aq) + 2H+(aq) -> H2O(l) + CO2(g)}\)

If none of these happens, all ions cancel and there is no reaction. That is a legitimate answer — write "NR" and say why.

Charge balances too. Sum the charges on each side; they must be equal (not necessarily zero). In \(\ce{Mg(s) + 2H+(aq) -> Mg^2+(aq) + H2(g)}\): \(0+2(+1)=+2\) on the left, \(+2+0=+2\) on the right. ✓

6. Alloys

An alloy is a mixture (not a compound) of a metal with one or more other elements, and it keeps metallic bonding: cations in a sea of delocalized electrons. That's why alloys stay conductive, malleable, and lustrous.

SubstitutionalInterstitial
StructureAdded atoms replace host atoms in the latticeAdded atoms squeeze into holes between host atoms
Size requirementAtomic radii within about 15% of each otherAdded atom much smaller (C, N, H, B)
ExamplesBrass (\(\ce{Cu}+\ce{Zn}\)), bronze (\(\ce{Cu}+\ce{Sn}\)), sterling silver (\(\ce{Ag}+\ce{Cu}\))Steel (\(\ce{Fe}+\ce{C}\)), stainless partly interstitial
Main effectModest hardening; properties close to the host metalMarked hardening and loss of malleability; density changes little

Why alloys are harder than pure metals

In a pure metal, identical atoms sit in neat, uniform layers that can slide past each other when stress is applied — this is what makes pure metals soft and malleable. Different-sized atoms disrupt the regular lattice, so the layers no longer line up and can't slip easily. Result: harder, stronger, less malleable.

Free-response phrasing: "The different-sized atoms distort the regular arrangement of the lattice, which prevents layers of atoms from sliding past one another, so the alloy is harder than the pure metal." Say layers sliding — that's the phrase graders look for.

Two more consequences worth knowing. Conductivity drops slightly: the irregular lattice scatters the delocalized electrons, so brass conducts less well than pure copper. Melting behavior changes: a pure metal melts sharply at one temperature, while an alloy is a mixture and generally melts lower and over a range. Alloying can also raise corrosion resistance — chromium in stainless steel forms a thin protective oxide layer.

7. Mass spectrometry

A mass spectrometer ionizes atoms, accelerates them, and deflects them in a magnetic field. Lighter ions deflect more, so the instrument separates them by mass-to-charge ratio \((m/z)\). The output plot shows one peak per isotope: x-axis = mass, y-axis = relative abundance.

Reading a spectrum

  • Number of peaks = number of naturally occurring isotopes.
  • Peak position = isotope mass (≈ mass number).
  • Peak height = relative abundance. The average atomic mass falls between the lightest and heaviest isotope masses, pulled toward the most abundant one. (With exactly two isotopes it always lands nearest the taller peak.)
  • Isotopes have the same number of protons and electrons but different neutrons — identical chemistry, different mass.

Weighted average

\[\bar{A}=\sum_i f_i A_i \qquad\text{where } f_i=\text{fractional abundance},\ A_i=\text{isotope mass}\]

Convert percentages to decimals first. For chlorine:

\[\bar{A}=(0.7576)(34.969)+(0.2424)(36.966)=35.45\ \mathrm{amu}\]

Notice the answer sits near 35, not halfway to 37 — because \(\ce{^{35}Cl}\) dominates.

Working backwards for abundances

Two isotopes, unknown split: let \(x\) be the fraction of the lighter one, so \(1-x\) is the heavier.

\[x\,A_1+(1-x)\,A_2=\bar{A}\]

Boron:

\[x(10.013)+(1-x)(11.009)=10.81 \;\Longrightarrow\; x=\frac{0.199}{0.996}=0.200\]

So 20.0% \(\ce{^{10}B}\) and 80.0% \(\ce{^{11}B}\).

Quick check: the average must fall between the isotope masses, and closer to the more abundant one. If your answer is outside that range or gives a negative abundance, you flipped the two masses.

Lab 1 — Unknown chloride (gravimetric analysis)

The idea

You have an unknown metal chloride. Dissolve it, add excess silver nitrate, and every chloride ion precipitates as \(\ce{AgCl}\). Weigh the dry \(\ce{AgCl}\), work backwards to the mass of chloride in your original sample, and that gives the percent chloride — which identifies the compound.

\[\ce{Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq) -> AgCl(s)}\]

Procedure, and why each step exists

  1. Weigh the unknown accurately on an analytical balance. Every later number depends on this one.
  2. Dissolve in distilled water. Tap water contains chloride — it would inflate your result.
  3. Add \(\ce{AgNO3}\) in excess. Excess guarantees complete precipitation. Test for completeness by adding another drop to the clear supernatant: no new cloudiness means you're done.
  4. Digest / let stand, often warm. Small crystals dissolve and redeposit onto larger ones, giving a coarser precipitate that filters without passing through the paper.
  5. Filter and wash with cold distilled water to remove trapped soluble ions (\(\ce{NO3-}\), the spectator cation). Cold, because \(\ce{AgCl}\) is very slightly soluble.
  6. Dry to constant mass. Weigh, dry again, reweigh; when two masses agree, all water is gone. Keep \(\ce{AgCl}\) out of direct light — it photodecomposes to grey silver metal.

The calculation

A \(0.745\ \mathrm{g}\) sample of an unknown chloride \(\ce{MCl}\) yields \(1.433\ \mathrm{g}\) of dry \(\ce{AgCl}\). Identify M.

\[n_{\ce{AgCl}}=\frac{1.433\ \mathrm{g}}{143.32\ \mathrm{g/mol}}=0.01000\ \mathrm{mol}=n_{\ce{Cl-}}\] \[m_{\ce{Cl}}=(0.01000)(35.45)=0.3545\ \mathrm{g} \qquad \%\,\ce{Cl}=\frac{0.3545}{0.745}\times100=47.6\%\] \[M_{\ce{MCl}}=\frac{0.745\ \mathrm{g}}{0.01000\ \mathrm{mol}}=74.5\ \mathrm{g/mol} \qquad M_{\ce{M}}=74.5-35.45=39.1\ \mathrm{g/mol}\]

\(39.1\ \mathrm{g/mol}\) is potassium, so the unknown is \(\ce{KCl}\).

For comparison: \(\ce{LiCl}\) is 83.6% Cl, \(\ce{NaCl}\) 60.7%, \(\ce{KCl}\) 47.6%, \(\ce{CaCl2}\) 63.9%. A percent-chloride value maps to one compound.

Lab 2 — Empirical formula of a hydrate

The idea

A hydrate is an ionic compound with water molecules locked into its crystal lattice in a fixed ratio, written \(\ce{MgSO4.7H2O}\). Heating drives off the water, leaving the anhydrous salt. Mass lost = mass of water. From there it's a two-substance empirical formula problem.

\[\ce{MgSO4.$x$H2O(s) ->[\Delta] MgSO4(s) + $x$H2O(g)}\]

Procedure

  1. Mass the empty crucible and lid, after pre-heating and cooling them, to burn off any residue or moisture already present.
  2. Add hydrate; mass again. Hydrate mass = difference.
  3. Heat gently, then strongly, lid ajar. Gentle first so the sample doesn't spatter out; ajar so steam escapes but solid doesn't.
  4. Cool in a desiccator before massing. A hot crucible creates convection currents that make the balance read low, and the anhydrous salt is hygroscopic — it reabsorbs water from the air.
  5. Heat again, cool, remass until two consecutive masses agree (constant mass). That's your proof all water is gone.

The calculation

\(4.94\ \mathrm{g}\) of \(\ce{MgSO4.$x$H2O}\) is heated to \(2.41\ \mathrm{g}\) of anhydrous \(\ce{MgSO4}\). Find \(x\).

\[m_{\ce{H2O}}=4.94-2.41=2.53\ \mathrm{g}\] \[n_{\ce{H2O}}=\frac{2.53}{18.02}=0.1404\ \mathrm{mol}\qquad n_{\ce{MgSO4}}=\frac{2.41}{120.37}=0.02002\ \mathrm{mol}\] \[x=\frac{0.1404}{0.02002}=7.01\ \approx\ 7\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ce{MgSO4.7H2O}\]

Percent water by mass \(=\dfrac{2.53}{4.94}\times100=51.2\%\). Worth reporting — it's a common follow-up question.
